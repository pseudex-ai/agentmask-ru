# B3 — Benchmark honesty: primary literature and practice

Excavated 2026-09-20. Scope is narrow by brief: the principles, statistics and governance that make a benchmark honest and hard to game, with the primary source behind each, so the public PII-gateway benchmark's design doc can cite them. Not a survey of which benchmarks exist.

Grades: [A] peer-reviewed or independently reproduced · [B] single primary (vendor post, preprint, primary code/docs) · [C] position / guideline · [D] blog-asserted.

Two corrections to the brief before anything else:

- SWE-bench Verified is an **OpenAI + Princeton (SWE-bench authors)** artefact, not Anthropic's. OpenAI: "Together with the authors of SWE-bench, we are releasing SWE-bench Verified" (openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified). It is also the cleanest precedent in the whole file for a vendor-authored benchmark, because the same vendor **retired it in public** eighteen months later (§1.5).
- "TOST in LLM evals" has **no located precedent**. The precedent for equivalence/non-inferiority claims about an AI system lives in biostatistics (Schuirmann 1987 → Lakens 2017) and in clinical AI trials (MASAI, Lancet Oncology 2023 / Lancet 2026), plus a Bayesian ML analogue (Benavoli et al., JMLR 2017, ROPE). The design doc should say so rather than cite a non-existent LLM precedent.

## 1. Sources, by objective

### 1.1 Contamination and construction validity — what a benchmark card must disclose

**Bean et al., "Measuring what Matters: Construct Validity in Large Language Model Benchmarks"** — NeurIPS 2025 Datasets & Benchmarks. https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04703 · [A]
Mechanism: a systematic review of 445 benchmark papers by 29 reviewers, coded on 21 items, producing eight recommendations each with a checklist. The core claim is that most benchmarks never argue the chain "phenomenon → task → items → metric → claim", so a score cannot be read as a measurement of anything.
Verbatim: "With a team of 29 expert reviewers, we conduct a systematic review of 445 LLM benchmarks from leading conferences in natural language processing and machine learning. Across the reviewed articles, we find patterns related to the measured phenomena, tasks, and scoring metrics which undermine the validity of the resulting claims."
Key numbers (all over the 445 reviewed): "Once the responses were scored, 16.0% used uncertainty estimates or statistical tests to compare the results." · "27.0% of reviewed benchmarks used convenience sampling" · "17.1% of reviewed benchmarks use at least one of [random or stratified sampling]" · "only about half (53.4%) of reviewed benchmarks justify why they are a valid measure of an important phenomenon" · "47.5%" generate synthetic examples to increase size · "Less than 10% of benchmarks used complete real-world tasks".
The eight recommendations, verbatim headings: "3.1 Define the phenomenon · 3.2 Measure the phenomenon and only the phenomenon · 3.3 Construct a representative dataset for the task · 3.4 Acknowledge limitations of reusing datasets · 3.5 Prepare for contamination · 3.6 Use statistical methods to compare models · 3.7 Conduct an error analysis · 3.8 Justify construct validity".
Two sentences that bear directly on this project:
- On contamination-by-optimisation, which is the vendor-author risk stated formally: "When a benchmark is widely used, the progressive effort to improve on that benchmark means that newly developed techniques will be specifically suited to solving that task. Over time, this selection of methods can lead to overfitting, similar to the repeated use of a validation set, effectively contaminating the benchmark."
- On confounds: "If a benchmark uses strict formats or complex instructions, test those skills independently and allow retries to distinguish formatting proficiency from task performance. If LLMs are used to parse original model responses, the extractor LLM should be validated to avoid introducing new biases or performance artifacts."

**Reuel et al., "BetterBench: Assessing AI Benchmarks, Uncovering Issues, and Establishing Best Practices"** — NeurIPS 2024 D&B (Advances in NeurIPS 37, pp. 21763–21813). https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.12990 · site with living database + checklist: https://betterbench.stanford.edu · [A]
Mechanism: 46 criteria across a five-stage lifecycle (design, implementation, documentation, maintenance, retirement), scored 0/5/10/15 on 24 benchmarks. Produces a "checklist for minimum quality assurance" (Appendix I) for developers.
Verbatim: "we develop an assessment framework considering 46 best practices across an AI benchmark's lifecycle and evaluate 24 AI benchmarks against it. We find that there exist large quality differences and that commonly used benchmarks suffer from significant issues. We further find that most benchmarks do not report statistical significance of their results nor allow for their results to be easily replicated."
Key numbers: "17 out of 24 benchmarks do not provide easy-to-run scripts to replicate the results reported in the initial paper" · "14 out of 24 benchmarks did not perform multiple evaluations of the same model or report statistical significance" · MMLU scored lowest (weighted avg 5.5), GPQA 11.0.
Criteria this project should lift verbatim into its card: re-run with "different random seeds or sampling temperatures, and report the mean and variance"; "minimize the risks of contamination and gamification by including a globally unique identifier or encrypting evaluation instances"; provide a replication script; name a feedback channel and contact; involve domain experts in design. Construct validity is named as an open challenge (their §8), which is exactly the gap Bean et al. then filled.

**Gebru et al., "Datasheets for Datasets"** — CACM 64(12), 2021 (arXiv 1803.09010, 2018). · [C] guideline, widely adopted
Mechanism: a fixed question list under seven headings (Motivation, Composition, Collection process, Preprocessing/cleaning/labeling, Uses, Distribution, Maintenance). The Motivation section is where a conflict of interest is disclosed as a matter of course, not as a confession. Questions, as reproduced by adopters: "Who created the dataset and on behalf of which entity?" · "Who funded the creation of the dataset? If there is an associated grant, provide the name of the grantor and the grant name and number." · "For what purpose was the dataset created?"
Relevance: the NeurIPS D&B checklist and the HF dataset card both descend from this list; a benchmark card that answers the Motivation questions honestly has already disclosed the vendor relationship.

**Mitchell et al., "Model Cards for Model Reporting"** — FAT* 2019 (arXiv 1810.03993). · [A]
Verbatim: "Model cards include information about the context of the model, as well as model performance results disaggregated by different unitary and intersectional population groups." and "Results on several different evaluation datasets will additionally aid potential users".
Relevance: the *disaggregation* principle. For this benchmark it means per-type (NAME/LOC/PHONE/…), per-register (clean/chat/sloppy/voice) and per-family reporting, never one headline number — the same reason the project already prints "recall" and "coverage" as two lines.

**Liang et al., "Holistic Evaluation of Language Models" (HELM)** — TMLR 2023 (arXiv 2211.09110). · [A]
Verbatim: "We believe holistic evaluation involves three elements: Broad coverage and recognition of incompleteness. … Multi-metric measurement … Standardization." and "Different benchmarks make different decisions on what to prioritize, how to make these decisions, and to what extent these processes are made clear in presenting the benchmark."
Relevance: "recognition of incompleteness" is a disclosure obligation — the card states what the benchmark does NOT cover (e.g. no bare-city masking by design, no CVC type), so a low number on an excluded axis is read as a policy, not a hole. The project's MASSIVE `place_name` 0.1% is exactly this case.

**NeurIPS Evaluations & Datasets track — 2026 Call for Papers and FAQ.** https://neurips.cc/Conferences/2026/CallForEvaluationsDatasets · [C]
Verbatim: "Datasets and code must be properly hosted, accessible, and clearly documented upon submission; these are essential submission requirements." · "New datasets should remain available long-term. Therefore, they should be hosted on a dedicated ML hosting site (Dataverse, Kaggle, Hugging Face, or OpenML)" · "Benchmarks or datasets that include a collection of new or existing datasets must also ensure that the underlying datasets are compliant with the same guidelines (eg, availability, croissant files, etc.)". The main-track checklist (which D&B submissions also answer) carries the "error bars / statistical significance" and "compute" items.
Relevance: a Croissant metadata file, long-term hosting, and a licence are table stakes for a benchmark anyone else will cite.

**Xu et al., "Benchmark Data Contamination of Large Language Models: A Survey"** — arXiv 2406.04244, 2024. · [B] survey preprint
Mechanism: defines BDC as "exposure of a large language model to benchmark data during the training process leads to distorted evaluation results" and lays out a four-level severity ladder (semantic → information → data → label), plus detection (n-gram overlap, perplexity, guided prompting, canary strings) and mitigation (dynamic/time-windowed benchmarks, private held-out sets, perturbation) families.
Relevance: gives the taxonomy the card should use when it states "contamination status: X".

**Balloccu et al., "Leak, Cheat, Repeat: Data Contamination and Evaluation Malpractices in Closed-Source LLMs"** — EACL 2024 main, pp. 67–93. https://aclanthology.org/2024.eacl-long.5 · [A]
Mechanism: "indirect data leaking" — evaluating a closed model through its web UI leaks the test set into the vendor's training data. Leak severity = k/n exposed samples; bands Low <5%, Moderate-low 5–50%, Moderate-high 50–95%, High >95%. Also catalogues evaluation malpractices (no seeds/temperature, cherry-picked prompts, unreported model version).
Verbatim: "researchers and stakeholders may later inadvertently evaluate LLMs on the same data they were trained on."
Relevance: a PII benchmark whose harness calls a vendor's masker *over the network* leaks its test set to that vendor exactly this way; the card must say whether the masker under test saw the items before, and the harness should support a fresh private split.

**LiveBench** — White et al., arXiv 2406.19314 (ICLR 2025). · [A]
Verbatim: "the first benchmark that (1) contains frequently-updated questions from recent information sources, (2) scores answers automatically according to objective ground-truth values … Questions will be added and updated on a monthly basis, and we will release new tasks and harder versions of tasks over time".
**LiveCodeBench** — Jain et al., arXiv 2403.07974 (ICLR 2025). · [A]
Verbatim: "we associate the contest date D for each problem. The release date allows us the measure the performance of LLMs over different time windows by filtering problems based on whether the problem release date falls within a time window (referred to as 'scrolling' through time). … for a new model and the corresponding cutoff date … we can measure the performance of the model on benchmark problems released after the cutoff date."
Relevance: the time-window design is the mechanism that makes contamination *checkable* rather than *asserted*: every item carries a creation date; a masker's score is reported on items created after the masker's last release. For a rule-based masker whose vendor authors the benchmark, the analogue is: items created after the masker version freeze, by a generator whose pools the vendor did not see.

**Humanity's Last Exam** — Phan et al., arXiv 2501.14249; published in Nature 649, 1139–1146 (2026). · [A]
Mechanism, verbatim: "We publicly release these questions, while maintaining a private test set of held out questions to assess model overfitting." and — critically for the "filter is the model" question — "These low scores are partially by design – the dataset collection process (Section 3.1) attempts to filter out questions that existing models can answer correctly. … We choose to leave these questions in the dataset as a natural component instead of strongly adversarially filtering." Governance: ~1,000 contributors, 500+ institutions, prize pool ($5,000 per top-50 question, $500 for the next 500), multiple review rounds, and the leaderboard does NOT report private-set numbers ("At this time, we do not report any model performance metrics on the private held-out set." — Scale SEAL page).
Relevance: HLE is model-in-the-loop selection *declared on the card*, with its consequence stated ("the true capability floor" is not what the number says). That is the honest form of the pattern.

### 1.2 Selection bias in construction — "the filter is the model", and what it is called

**OpenAI, "Introducing SWE-bench Verified"** (Aug 2024). https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified · [B]
Verbatim: "We see that 38.3% of samples were flagged for underspecified problem statements, and 61.1% were flagged for unit tests that may unfairly mark valid solutions as incorrect. Overall, our annotation process resulted in 68.3% of SWE-bench samples being filtered out due to underspecification, unfair unit tests, or other issues. As discussed previously, this filtering process is likely to be overzealous". Also: "we filter out any sample … where either the problem statement or the FAIL_TO_PASS unit tests have an ensemble label of 2 or above in severity … equivalent to filtering out samples where any single annotator of three has flagged an issue". And a disclosed side effect: "SWE-bench Lite subsampled the original dataset to make the benchmark easier, whereas SWE-bench Verified …" leaves "fewer than 10% of issues estimated to take longer than an hour".
Mechanism: human filtering with a *stated* rule, three independent annotators, annotations released for all 1,699 items so anyone can re-slice. That release is what made a vendor-authored subset credible.

**OpenAI, "Why SWE-bench Verified no longer measures frontier coding capabilities"** (23 Feb 2026). https://openai.com/index/why-we-no-longer-evaluate-swe-bench-verified · [B]
Verbatim: "We conducted an audit of 138 SWE-bench Verified problems that OpenAI o3 did not consistently solve over 64 independent runs. Each case was independently reviewed by at least six experienced software engineers. … We found that 59.4% of the 138 problems contained material issues in test design and/or problem description". On contamination: "when GPT‑5.2 solved 31 tasks we identified to be almost impossible to solve" and, across GPT‑5.2, Claude Opus 4.5 and Gemini 3 Flash, an automated elicitation harness recovered gold patches "verbatim". Conclusion: "improvements on SWE-bench Verified no longer reflect meaningful improvements in models' real-world software development abilities. Instead, they increasingly reflect how much the model was exposed to the benchmark at training time. This is why we have stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified scores, and we recommend that other model developers do so too." Two design lessons stated verbatim: "benchmarks sourced from publicly available material carry contamination risk" and "automated scoring is tricky to get right; perfect test cases should fully verify correct functionality, being both agnostic to specific unimportant implementation details and also robust to shortcut solutions."
Relevance: the "too narrow / too wide" test failure is structurally the project's own "required-field spec nobody's scorer read" and "type-blind overlap" defects. A vendor publicly retiring its own benchmark is the strongest available precedent that self-authorship can be credible — provided the audit method (138 tasks, 64 runs, ≥6 reviewers, re-verification of every flag) is published.

**OpenAI, "Separating signal from noise in coding evaluations"** (8 Jul 2026). https://openai.com/index/separating-signal-from-noise-coding-evaluations · [B]
Verbatim: "Through a detailed audit, we find widespread task issues in SWE-Bench Pro and estimate that ~30% of the tasks are broken." Relevance: one vendor auditing another vendor's replacement benchmark, with the number published. Cross-vendor audit is a governance mechanism, not just a courtesy.

**Aleithan et al., "SWE-Bench+: Enhanced Coding Benchmark for LLMs"** — arXiv 2410.06992 (2024); AIware 2026 benchmark track. · [A] (the 2026 version is peer-reviewed; numbers below from the 2024 preprint and the 2026 abstract)
Verbatim (2024): "33.47% of patches have solution leakage, where the fix is directly or indirectly revealed in the issue report or comments; and (2) 24.70% of successful patches are suspicious due to weak test cases that fail to detect incorrect, incomplete, or irrelevant fixes. Filtering out these problematic instances drops SWE-Agent + GPT-4's resolution rate from 12.47% to 4.58%." (2026): "60.83% of commonly resolved issues contain solution leakage and 77.88% are problematic overall".
Relevance: "solution leakage" is the formal name for the item carrying its own answer — the analogue of a PII item whose surrounding cue words are the masker's own trigger list.

**Bras et al., "Adversarial Filters of Dataset Biases" (AFLite)** — ICML 2020, PMLR 119. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/bras20a.html · [A]
Mechanism: model-based filtering of items a linear probe over a strong model's features can solve; the *filter is a model by design*, and the paper is explicit about the consequence: "filtering results in a large drop in model performance (e.g., from 92% to 62% for …)". "performance is lowest when using the strongest model (RoBERTa) for learning feature representations."
Relevance: this is the recognised name for the pattern in the brief's item (2), run in the opposite direction. **"Adversarial filtering" / "model-in-the-loop selection"** (Zellers et al. 2018 SWAG/HellaSwag; Bras et al. 2020; Kiela et al. 2021 Dynabench) selects items *against* a model and produces model-specific artefacts; the project's voice-benchmark defect selected items *for* the detector's own signals. Same mechanism, opposite sign, same remedy: the selection rule must be independent of the system under test, and the card must state it.

**Lewis, Stenetorp, Riedel, "Question and Answer Test-Train Overlap in Open-Domain Question Answering Datasets"** — EACL 2021, pp. 1000–1008. https://aclanthology.org/2021.eacl-main.86 · [A]
Verbatim: "We find that 60-70% of test-time answers are also present somewhere in the training sets. We also find that 30% of test-set questions have a near-duplicate paraphrase in their corresponding training sets." and "58-71% of test answers also occur somewhere in the training data, demonstrating that the majority of the test data does not probe for answer generalization."
Relevance: this is the formal name for the brief's item (3), "generator pools ⊂ system vocabulary": **test–train overlap**, and the remedy is theirs — stratify the test set by overlap and report both strata. The project's own number ("0 of 2172 values inside the leased lists, 8 of 272 outside (2.9%)") is exactly this stratification and should be published in that form.

**Yao et al., "τ-bench"** — arXiv 2406.12045 (2024) · [B]; **Barres et al., "τ²-bench"** arXiv 2506.07982 (2025) · [B]; **taubench.com, "τ³-Bench: Fixing Airline + Retail"** (Feb 2026) · [B]; **Cuadron et al., "SABER: Small Actions, Big Errors"** arXiv 2512.07850, ICLR 2026 workshop · [B].
Mechanism: τ-bench's own construction was model-in-the-loop — verbatim: "iteratively until we are certain no ambiguities exist (see Figure 7 in § A, where we run each τ-retail task with > 40 gpt-4-turbo trials and check all tasks with zero or low success rates)". The failure breakdown admits "4 of them are caused by user instruction typo or ambiguity (and then fixed)". Two years later an *external* team (Amazon, SABER) "systematically identified annotation errors and underspecified tasks in the original benchmark" and Sierra "audited and fixed 50+ tasks across the airline and retail domains … incorrect expected actions, ambiguous user instructions, impossible constraints, and missing fallback behaviors" (tau2-bench README: "75+ fixes"). Versioning discipline, verbatim from the README: "results produced with tau2-bench < 1.0.1 are not comparable with >= 1.0.1, and affected leaderboard submissions have been re-graded. Old results files can be re-scored with `tau2 evaluate-trajs --fresh-tasks`; to reproduce pre-fix behavior, pin the `pre-v1.0.1` tag."
Relevance: (a) validating items by "does the model pass" filters toward what the reference model can do — declare it; (b) the fixes came from an outside audit, and the vendor accepted them with a version bump and re-grading. That is the governance shape to copy.

**Formal names for the pattern, with the field they come from:**
- *Test–train overlap* (Lewis et al. 2021, NLP) — generator vocabulary shared with the system's dictionaries.
- *Adversarial filtering / model-in-the-loop data collection* (Zellers 2018; Bras 2020; Kiela 2021, NLP) — selection rule computed from a model.
- *Solution leakage* (Aleithan et al. 2024, SE) — the item carries its own answer.
- *Criterion contamination* (I-O psychometrics, Brogden & Taylor 1950 lineage; SIOP "Criterion Theory and Development": "Criterion Contamination – Introducing extraneous elements into the [criterion]") — the ground-truth measure is influenced by the predictor. The closest formal name for "the benchmark's ground truth is what the detector says". [C]
- *Validation-set reuse / adaptive overfitting* (Bean et al. 3.5, quoted above) — the vendor's iteration against its own benchmark.
- *Convenience sampling* (Bean et al.: 27.0% of benchmarks) — items chosen because they were at hand.

### 1.3 Statistics for benchmark claims

**Miller, "Adding Error Bars to Evals: A Statistical Approach to Language Model Evaluations"** — Anthropic, arXiv 2411.00640 (Nov 2024). Read in full. · [B] (single-author preprint; formulas are standard econometrics — Abadie et al. QJE 2022 for clustering)
Five recommendations, verbatim: "1. Computing standard errors of the mean using the Central Limit Theorem 2. When questions are drawn in related groups, computing clustered standard errors 3. Reducing variance by resampling answers and by analyzing next-token probabilities 4. When two models are being compared, conducting statistical inference on the question-level paired differences, rather than the population-level summary statistics 5. Using power analysis to determine whether an eval (or a random subsample) is capable of testing a hypothesis of interest".
Formulas:
- SE_CLT = sqrt(Var(s)/n) (eq. 1); Bernoulli special case sqrt(s̄(1−s̄)/n) (eq. 2); "incorrectly estimates all of its standard errors using SE_Bernoulli, even when s_i takes fractional values, such as with an F1 score. In these cases, SE_Bernoulli will tend to be conservative (too wide)".
- Clustered (eq. 4): SE_clustered = [ SE²_CLT + (1/n²) Σ_c Σ_i Σ_{j≠i} (s_ic − s̄)(s_jc − s̄) ]^{1/2}. Real numbers on Anthropic models, verbatim Table 4: DROP clustered/naive ratio 3.05, RACE-H 1.10, MGSM 1.88 — "clustered standard errors can be over 3X larger than naive standard errors."
- Paired (eq. 7): SE_paired = sqrt(Var(s_A − s_B)/n), equivalently sqrt(SE_A² + SE_B² − 2·SE_A·SE_B·Corr(s_A,s_B)); paired-and-clustered (eq. 8) computed directly on the differences. "paired differences represent a 'free' reduction in estimator variance"; worked case at correlation 0.5: variance reduced by 1/3.
- Power (eq. 9): n = (z_{α/2} + z_β)² (ω² + σ²_A/K_A + σ²_B/K_B) / δ². Worked example: δ = 0.03, α = 0.05, power 0.8, ω² = 1/9 → n ≈ 969; "suggest that new evals should contain at least 1,000 questions in order to have good signaling ability." Inverse (eq. 10) gives the minimum detectable effect for a fixed n; at n = 198 raising K from 1 to 10 moved the MDE "from 13.2% to 7.5%".
- Resampling: with K answers per question, "Going from K = 1 … to K = 2, the total variance is reduced by 1/3"; upper limit 2/3 in the uniform-difficulty example. "Note that computing a pooled standard error across all KN answers will be inconsistent".
- "Don't touch the thermostat": lowering temperature can triple the variance of conditional means (1/12 → 1/4) and inject bias.
- On the bootstrap: "we regard bootstrapping as unnecessary unless a complicated sampling scheme or estimator is being used."
- Reporting format (Tables 2, 3, 5): number of questions, number of clusters, SE in parentheses beneath each mean; for comparisons, the difference, its SE, a 95% CI and the score correlation.
Relevance: the project's *case* is the cluster (several spans per case, several cases per family); the *family* may be the right cluster when items in a family are generated from one template. Report which.

**Bowyer, Aitchison, Ivanova, "Position: Don't Use the CLT in LLM Evals With Fewer Than a Few Hundred Datapoints"** — ICML 2025 Spotlight (PMLR 267). arXiv 2503.01747. · [A]
Verbatim: "particularly with smaller datasets, the CLT produces unreliable intervals that fail to achieve their target confidence level, also known as nominal coverage. This is true across a variety of settings: when evaluating a single model and when comparing two models, both with IID and clustered questions." · "We would, therefore, recommend using WS or Bayesian intervals in practice. Both methods are easy to apply. WS (and CP) are implemented in SciPy. The posterior for Beta-Bernoulli is available in closed form: P(θ|y_1:N) = Beta(1+Σy_i, 1+Σ(1−y_i))". Secondary summary of their sweep: CLT intervals fail "across all scenarios when n < 100".
Relevance: per-family and per-type slices of this benchmark will routinely have n < 100. Use Wilson (WS) or Clopper-Pearson (CP) per slice; reserve Miller's CLT/clustered machinery for the pooled comparison.

**Dietterich, "Approximate Statistical Tests for Comparing Supervised Classification Learning Algorithms"** — Neural Computation 10(7):1895–1923, 1998. · [A]
Verbatim (abstract): "Two widely used statistical tests are shown to have high probability of type I error in certain situations and should never be used: a test for the difference of two proportions and a paired-differences t test based on taking several random train-test splits. … A fourth test, McNemar's test, is shown to have low type I error. … For algorithms that can be executed only once, McNemar's test is the only test with acceptable type I error."
Relevance: this is the classical warrant for McNemar on paired binary outcomes over one fixed test set — precisely the shape of "did arm A and arm B both mask item i". The "difference of two proportions" test the paper forbids is the naive one a vendor is tempted to run.

**Dror, Baumer, Shlomov, Reichart, "The Hitchhiker's Guide to Testing Statistical Significance in Natural Language Processing"** — ACL 2018, pp. 1383–1392. https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128 · [A]
Mechanism: a decision procedure for NLP: same test set → paired tests; check distributional assumptions; if the metric is not a simple mean (F1, BLEU) → paired bootstrap or approximate randomization; binary per-item outcomes → sign/McNemar. Relevance: the approved home for "paired bootstrap on F1" and "approximate randomization" (which the i2b2 organisers used, see §1.4).

**Card et al., "With Little Power Comes Great Responsibility"** — EMNLP 2020, pp. 9263–9274. · [A]
Verbatim: "if an experiment's test set is small, the minimum detectable effect (MDE) size may be large: only large improvements will yield sufficiently powered comparisons (i.e., ≥80% power). If an experiment is badly underpowered, it cannot provide useful evidence that one model achieves slightly better performance than another". Table 2 (estimated MDE vs. typical reported gains): MRPC n=1,725 → MDE +1.62% while average reported improvement was +0.63%; SST-2 n=1,821 → MDE +1.02% vs +0.57%; WNLI n=147 → MDE +5.26%.
Relevance: the number the card must print next to every comparison is the MDE at the benchmark's n, and the card must say when a claimed difference is below it.

**Lakens, "Equivalence Tests: A Practical Primer for t Tests, Correlations, and Meta-Analyses"** — Social Psychological and Personality Science 8(4):355–362, 2017. DOI 10.1177/1948550617697177. · [A]
Verbatim: "Scientists should be able to provide support for the absence of a meaningful effect. Currently, researchers often incorrectly conclude an effect is absent based a nonsignificant result. A widely recommended approach within a frequentist framework is to test for equivalence. In equivalence tests, such as the two one-sided tests (TOST) …". Recipe: choose the smallest effect size of interest (SESOI) *before* seeing data; run two one-sided tests against −δ and +δ; equivalently, the (1−2α) — i.e. 90% for α=0.05 — confidence interval must lie entirely inside (−δ, +δ). p_TOST = max(p_lower, p_upper). Tooling: TOSTER (R), jamovi module; in Python `statsmodels.stats.weightstats.ttost_paired` and, for a binomial proportion, `statsmodels.stats.proportion.binom_tost`.
Origin: Schuirmann, J. Pharmacokinetics & Biopharmaceutics 15:657–680, 1987 (FDA bioequivalence, 80–125% band).
Precedent for "no degradation" claims about an AI system: **MASAI** — Lång et al., Lancet Oncology 2023 (DOI 10.1016/S1470-2045(23)00298-X), "a randomised, controlled, non-inferiority, single-blinded, screening accuracy study"; and Gommers et al., Lancet Jan 2026: "Interval cancer rates were non-inferior with AI-supported screening compared with standard double reading (1.55 vs 1.76 per 1000; ratio 0.88, 95% CI 0.65–1.18)". [A] The margin was pre-registered; the claim is stated as a ratio with its CI against that margin — the exact form a "gateway does not degrade the agent" claim should take.
ML analogue: **Benavoli, Corani, Demšar, Zaffalon, "Time for a Change: a Tutorial for Comparing Multiple Classifiers Through Bayesian Analysis"** — JMLR 18(77):1–36, 2017. [A] Introduces the *region of practical equivalence* (ROPE) into classifier comparison: a difference is declared practically equivalent when the posterior mass inside ±δ exceeds a threshold. Same logic as TOST, Bayesian form; usable with the Beta-Bernoulli posterior Bowyer et al. recommend.
**No TOST/ROPE precedent in LLM-benchmark papers was found in this search**; state that in the design doc rather than imply one.

**Hanley & Lippman-Hand, "If nothing goes wrong, is everything all right? Interpreting zero numerators"** — JAMA 249(13):1743–1745, 1983. · [A]
Verbatim (as quoted by Hanley's own later summary): "if none of n patients showed the event about which we are concerned, we can be 95% confident that the chance of this event is at most 3 in n (i.e. 3/n). In other words, the upper 95% confidence limit of a 0/n rate is approximately 3/n." Derivation: (1−p)^n = 0.05 → n·ln(1−p) = ln 0.05 ≈ −2.996. Exact form is the Clopper-Pearson (1934) one-sided bound; with zero errors the lower bound on precision is p_lower = α^{1/n}.
Computed for this project (α = 0.05 one-sided, zero errors observed): certify precision ≥ 99.0% needs n = 299; ≥ 99.4% needs n = 498; ≥ 99.5% needs 598; ≥ 99.9% needs 2,995. 42 flawless phrases certify only ≥ 93.1%; 538 flawless phrases certify ≥ 99.44%. These reproduce the numbers already in the project's CLAUDE.md (498 and 93.1%), so the arithmetic there is right. For 1,922 spans at 99.4% observed (~12 errors) the exact two-sided 95% CP interval is [98.9%, 99.7%].
Relevance to the bootstrap defect: a percentile bootstrap over n identical outcomes resamples a degenerate empirical distribution — every resample has the same mean — so it returns a zero-width interval at any n and certifies any margin. The exact binomial bound does not collapse (0/n → 3/n). This is why the project's "resolution floor of 1/n" is a patch and Clopper-Pearson is the fix; Miller ("bootstrapping … unnecessary unless a complicated sampling scheme or estimator is being used") and Bowyer et al. (CP/Wilson in SciPy) both point the same way.

**pass^k and avg@k** — τ-bench (Yao et al. 2024), verbatim: "the community has defined the pass@k (pass at k) metric as the chance that at least one out of k i.i.d. task trials is successful … For real-world agent tasks requiring reliability and consistency like customer service, we propose a new metric – pass^k (pass hat k), defined as the chance that all k i.i.d. task trials are successful, averaged across tasks. Therefore, if a task is run for n trials and c of [them succeed, pass^k = C(c,k)/C(n,k) averaged over tasks]". Numbers: "Even for the best-performing gpt-4o function calling agent which has a > 60% average task success, pass^8 drops to < 25%." · [B]
Relevance: for a *gateway* the right reliability statistic is pass^k on the tool-call round trip (all k trials restore the real value), because one leaked call is a breach regardless of the other k−1. avg@k (mean over k trials) is Miller's K-resampling and reduces variance; pass^k is a different *claim* — reliability — and the two must not be swapped.

### 1.4 Negatives / control items and over-masking

**Pilán, Lison, Øvrelid, Papadopoulou, Sánchez, Batet, "The Text Anonymization Benchmark (TAB)"** — Computational Linguistics 48(4):1053–1101, Dec 2022, MIT Press. https://aclanthology.org/2022.cl-4.19 (arXiv 2202.00443). · [A]
This is the closest existing precedent for scoring a masker on both axes, and it names the "recall bought by masking everything" failure explicitly. Verbatim:
- "the goal of the anonymization process is not to detect all occurrences of predefined semantic categories, but to mask only entities that refer to the individual to be protected."
- "the generic NER model performed poorly w.r.t. precision: due to the large variety of general categories it considers, more than half of the tagged entities dit not need to be masked. A similar behavior can be observed for Presidio when enabling the quite general ORG category: recall sightly increases at the cost of a significantly lower precision."
- "This illustrates another limitation of NER-based systems, namely the fact that they incur in unnecessary masking that hampers the utility and readability of the protected outcomes. In summary, both systems offered a poor balance between recall/protection and precision/utility preservation."
- Metrics: "An entity-level recall on direct identifiers ER_di, An entity-level recall on quasi-identifiers ER_qi, A token-level weighted precision on both direct and quasi identifiers WP_di+qi." Precision is weighted by information content: "tokens that convey more information (or, equivalently, are more difficult to predict from the edited document) lead to a higher penalty on the precision measure."
- Why separate recalls: "although the standard, mention-level recall seems relatively good at first sight (around 0.7), a closer look a the entity-level recall over direct identifiers ER_di shows a much poorer performance (around 0.45). Since quasi-identifiers are typically much more frequent than direct identifiers, this poor performance on direct identifiers (which are the most harmful entities from a privacy perspective) is easy to miss if one conflates all identifiers in a single recall measure."
Numbers (Table 7, RoBERTa NER on dev): R_di+qi 0.910, ER_di 0.970, ER_qi 0.874, P 0.447, WP 0.531 — i.e. recall 0.91 with precision 0.45, the "mask everything" profile made visible only because precision was scored.

**Stubbs, Kotfila, Uzuner, "Automated systems for the de-identification of longitudinal clinical narratives: Overview of 2014 i2b2/UTHealth shared task Track 1"** — J Biomed Inform 58:S11–S19, 2015. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989908 · [A]
Verbatim: "We used precision (Eq. 1), recall (Eq. 2 and F-measure (Eq. 3) scores to evaluate the participants' results against the gold standard annotations. We checked the significance of the differences of the systems from each other using approximate randomization (Chinchor, 1992 and Noreen, 1989)." · "We evaluated all systems on both i2b2-PHI and HIPAA-PHI categories, both based on entity- and token-level annotations. We ranked each team based on their top performing system run in terms of micro-averaged entity-based F1 on i2b2-PHI categories." · "All the teams' scores are higher using this [token-level] evaluation metric, suggesting that some of the errors in the entity-based evaluations are from not capturing the entire PHI entity."
Also: "the 2014 i2b2/UTHealth shared task data were de-identified to a more strict standard than what HIPAA defines … using additional categories for PHI, such as professions, full dates".
Relevance: (a) the scoring script reports **four** numbers (entity/token × i2b2/HIPAA) and *ranks on the strictest*; (b) the sentence about token-level scores being higher "from not capturing the entire PHI entity" is the recall-vs-coverage gap, seen in 2014; (c) precision is scored at both levels, so over-redaction costs rank. Precedent for "publish the strict number as the headline, the lenient one beneath it".

**Röttger et al., "XSTest: A Test Suite for Identifying Exaggerated Safety Behaviours in Large Language Models"** — NAACL 2024, pp. 5377–5400. https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.301 · [A]
Verbatim: "XSTest comprises 250 safe prompts across ten prompt types that well-calibrated models should not refuse to comply with, and 200 unsafe prompts as contrasts that models, for most applications, should refuse." Construction principle: "we use diverse vocabulary and syntax within each prompt type. For example, we do not test for the same homonym twice". Design lineage: contrast sets "to better evaluate models' decision boundaries (Gardner et al., 2020)".
Relevance: the *contrast set* is the formal device for the project's `negatives` — each hard negative is a minimal edit of a positive, so the boundary is measured, not the vocabulary. XSTest's finding that "models with higher exaggerated safety generally performed better on genuinely unsafe prompts" is the safety-domain version of "recall bought by masking everything".

**Cui et al., "OR-Bench: An Over-Refusal Benchmark for Large Language Models"** — ICML 2025. arXiv 2405.20947. · [A]
Verbatim: "OR-Bench comprises 80,000 over-refusal prompts across 10 common rejection categories, a subset of around 1,000 hard prompts that are challenging even for state-of-the-art LLMs, and an additional 600 toxic prompts to prevent indiscriminate responses." Key number (secondary summary of the paper): correlation 0.89 between safety and over-refusal across 32 models.
Relevance: the "600 toxic prompts to prevent indiscriminate responses" is the structural rule — a negatives set is only honest if it ships *with* a positives set the same harness scores, so nobody can win the over-masking axis by masking nothing. Note also the construction caveat: OR-Bench's hard subset was "selected because they were rejected by multiple state-of-the-art models" — model-in-the-loop, declared.

Who names "recall bought by masking everything": TAB (2022) names it as "unnecessary masking that hampers the utility", XSTest (2023/24) as "exaggerated safety", OR-Bench (2024/25) as "over-refusal", i2b2 (2015) implicitly by scoring precision at entity level. No single canonical term; "over-masking" / "over-redaction" is used in the de-identification literature, "utility loss" in TAB.

### 1.5 Vendor-authored benchmarks and conflict of interest

**Singh et al. (Cohere, Princeton, Stanford, Waterloo, MIT, et al.), "The Leaderboard Illusion"** — NeurIPS 2025. arXiv 2504.20879. · [A]
Verbatim abstract: "We find that undisclosed private testing practices benefit a handful of providers who are able to test multiple variants before public release and retract scores if desired. We establish that the ability of these providers to choose the best score leads to biased Arena scores due to selective disclosure of performance results. At an extreme, we identify 27 private LLM variants tested by Meta in the lead-up to the Llama-4 release. … Providers like Google and OpenAI have received an estimated 19.2% and 20.4% of all data on the arena, respectively. In contrast, a combined 83 open-weight models have only received an estimated 29.7% of the total data. … even limited additional data can result in relative performance gains of up to 112% on the arena distribution, based on our conservative estimates."
Recommendations, verbatim: "Prohibit score retraction after submission. … Providers should also disclose the total number of private variants tested prior to public launch, including historical submissions, to contextualize their results." · "Establish transparent limits on the number of private variants per provider. … This should be enforced at a provider level" · "Establish clear and auditable model deprecation criteria." · "fairer sampling that is motivated by reducing uncertainty in rankings instead of being skewed towards giving proprietary models more battles."
Relevance: the governance rules a vendor-authored benchmark must adopt against *itself*: no retraction of its own submitted scores; publish how many internal variants were run against the benchmark before release; deprecation criteria written down.

**Epoch AI, "Clarifying the creation and use of the FrontierMath benchmark"** (23 Jan 2025). https://epoch.ai/latest/openai-and-frontiermath · [B]; timeline reconstruction [D].
Verbatim: "We clarify that OpenAI commissioned Epoch AI to produce 300 math questions for the FrontierMath benchmark. They own these and have access to the statements and solutions, except for a 50-question holdout set." · "As is typical of commissioned work, OpenAI retains ownership of these questions and has access to the problems and solutions, with the exception of a holdout set." Relevance: the precedent for *what makes a vendor benchmark distrusted*: disclosure after the score, not before; contributors unaware; the funder holding the answers. The remedy that restored partial trust — a holdout the vendor cannot see — is the one to adopt from day one.

**Scale AI, "SEAL Leaderboards"** (May 2024). https://scale.com/blog/leaderboard · [B]
Verbatim: "Scale's proprietary datasets will remain private and unpublished, ensuring they cannot be exploited or incorporated into model training data. We limit entries to the SEAL Leaderboards from AI developers who may have seen the specific prompt sets via API logging, ensuring unbiased evaluations. To ensure rigorous results and maintain accountability, we plan to collaborate with trusted third party organizations to help review our work." and "all funded and developed by Scale".
Relevance: three credibility levers stated by a for-profit evaluator: private items, exclusion of parties who may have seen them, and third-party review. The disclosure that matters and is *absent* from that post: Scale sells data-labelling to the labs it ranks; the design doc should require that kind of commercial-relationship line explicitly.

**Vals AI** — https://www.vals.ai (2026). · [D] (marketing copy)
Verbatim: "Unlike results released by model providers on these benchmarks, we applied a consistent methodology and prompt-template across models, ensuring an apples-to-apples comparison." · "We run all of our own evaluations and create many of our benchmarks in-house." · "the world's leading AI labs already rely on Vals to test their models before release". **Sierra / τ-bench** — vendor-authored (Sierra Research + Princeton), adopted by others. Credibility mechanisms observed: open code and tasks; a leaderboard that accepts outside submissions; external audit (SABER, Amazon) accepted and credited; versioned re-grading with a pin to reproduce old behaviour (quoted in §1.2). [B]

**Eriksson et al. (EC Joint Research Centre), "Can We Trust AI Benchmarks? An Interdisciplinary Review of Current Issues in AI Evaluation"** — AIES 2025; arXiv 2502.06559. · [A]
Verbatim: "benchmark practices are fundamentally shaped by cultural, commercial and competitive dynamics that often prioritise state-of-the-art performance at the expense of broader societal concerns." · quoting Gehrmann et al. 2023: an "incentive mismatch between conducting high-quality evaluations and publishing new models or modelling techniques" · "private businesses share of the biggest AI models has increased from 11% in 2010 to 96% in 2021 [Ahmed et al., 2023]" and "current benchmarking tasks … are especially well suited to fit AI models that have been developed within the industry".

### 1.6 Type-aware scoring in span tasks

**SemEval-2013 Task 9.1 evaluation / MUC-5 scoring** as implemented in **nervaluate** (MantisAI; README). https://github.com/MantisAI/nervaluate · [B] (the code); the schemes trace to Chinchor & Sundheim 1993 (MUC-5) and Segura-Bedmar et al. 2013 (SemEval Task 9) [A].
Verbatim definitions: "Strict — exact boundary surface string match and entity type · Exact — exact boundary match over the surface string, regardless of the type · Partial — partial boundary match over the surface string, regardless of the type · Type — some overlap between the system tagged entity and the gold annotation is required". Five outcomes: "Correct (COR) … Incorrect (INC) … Partial (PAR) … Missing (MIS) … Spurious (SPU)". POSSIBLE = COR + INC + PAR + MIS; ACTUAL = COR + INC + PAR + SPU. Exact-match precision = COR/ACT; partial-match precision = (COR + 0.5×PAR)/ACT.
The scenario table shows exactly where overlap-only over-credits — verbatim rows: "IV | DRUG | propranolol | BRAND | propranolol | INC | COR | COR | INC" (right span, wrong type: *Exact* and *Partial* call it correct; *Strict* and *Type* do not) and "V | DRUG | warfarin | DRUG | of warfarin | COR | PAR | INC | INC" (right type, wrong boundary). A scorer that reports only "partial" or "exact" therefore credits a СНИЛС masked as a street; only "strict" (boundary AND type) refuses it. The README's own example: the same predictions score strict 0.40 vs partial 0.70.
Also note the caveat in the README: "if multiple true entities of the same label overlap a prediction, the match is resolved by closest boundaries."

**seqeval** (Nakayama 2018; README). https://github.com/chakki-works/seqeval · [B]
Verbatim: "This is well-tested by using the Perl script conlleval" · "seqeval supports the two evaluation modes … default … strict. The default mode is compatible with conlleval. … In strict mode, the inputs are evaluated according to the specified schema." The README's minimal example shows the *same* prediction scoring F1 1.00 in default mode and 0.00 in strict IOB2 mode — the tagging-scheme leniency is itself a hidden credit. CoNLL-style scoring is entity-level exact match with type (= nervaluate "strict"); it never awards partial credit.

**Precedents that separate "found" from "fully covered":**
- TAB (2022): entity-level recall counts an entity only "if and only if the anonymization model manages to completely mask all of its mentions" — coverage across mentions, not per-span overlap. [A]
- i2b2 2014 (2015): token-level vs entity-level reported side by side, with the gap attributed to "not capturing the entire PHI entity". [A]
- MUC/SemEval "partial" vs "strict/exact" (1993/2013): the PAR bucket *is* the "found but not fully covered" count, if it is reported rather than folded into a 0.5 credit. [A]
- nervaluate summary tables print all four schemes at once; the honest presentation is the strict column as headline and the partial column labelled as leak/overlap. [B]

## 2. Honesty checklist for the benchmark card (18 items)

Each item names the disclosure or mechanism, then the source that warrants it.

1. **Phenomenon defined before items exist**, including a negative definition ("a bare city is not personal data under 152-ФЗ art. 3"), and sub-phenomena (masking, tool-call round trip, over-masking) scored separately. — Bean et al. 2025 §3.1 [A].
2. **Chain of validity written out**: phenomenon → task → item source → sampling rule → metric → claim, with the trade-offs named (why JSON tool calls, why four registers). — Bean et al. §3.8 [A]; HELM "recognition of incompleteness" [A].
3. **Selection rule independence stated**: the rule that admits an item into the corpus must be computable without the system under test, and the card says what it is (e.g. "any digit or numeral", not "any street-type word"). Where a model or the vendor's detector WAS in the loop (adversarial filtering, difficulty tiers), say so and state the consequence. — Bras et al. 2020 (AFLite) [A]; HLE §3.1 declaration [A]; τ-bench "> 40 gpt-4-turbo trials" [B].
4. **Test–train / generator–vocabulary overlap measured and stratified**: publish the share of items whose values fall inside the vendor's own dictionaries, and score both strata separately. — Lewis et al. 2021 [A]; project's own "0 of 2172 inside / 8 of 272 outside" is the number to print.
5. **Solution leakage audit**: no item may carry the answer in its cue (the generator's cue words must not be the detector's cue list). Report the audit method and the exclusion count. — Aleithan et al. 2024/2026 [A]; OpenAI SWE-bench Verified 68.3% filtered [B].
6. **Contrast (negative) set shipped with the positive set, scored by the same harness, of stated size**, so over-masking is measured and cannot be won by masking nothing. — XSTest 250/200 [A]; OR-Bench 80k/1k/600 [A]; TAB precision [A].
7. **Two recall lines, never merged**: type-aware strict recall (boundary AND type) as headline; type-blind overlap reported separately and labelled "leak-only". — nervaluate/SemEval-2013 scenario IV [A/B]; project's own СНИЛС-as-street case.
8. **Coverage as a third line**: an entity counts as protected only if all its mentions/tokens are masked. — TAB entity-level recall [A]; i2b2 entity vs token tables [A].
9. **Precision weighted by harm/information**, or at minimum precision at entity level over the negatives, so a stray mask on a track number costs rank. — TAB WP_di+qi [A]; i2b2 ranks on strict entity F1 [A].
10. **Uncertainty on every number**: n and cluster count printed; SE in parentheses; Wilson or Clopper-Pearson for any slice with n < a few hundred; clustered SE at the case/family level for pooled numbers. — Miller 2024 Tables 2–3 [B]; Bowyer et al. 2025 [A]; Bean et al. "16.0%" [A]; BetterBench "14 of 24" [A].
11. **Comparisons are paired** on identical items, with the difference, its SE, 95% CI, correlation, and a McNemar count table (b, c) for binary outcomes. — Miller §4.2 Table 5 [B]; Dietterich 1998 [A]; Dror et al. 2018 [A].
12. **Minimum detectable effect printed next to every comparison**, from the benchmark's own n; any difference below it is labelled "not resolvable here". — Card et al. 2020 [A]; Miller eq. 10 [B].
13. **"No effect" claims use an equivalence test with a pre-registered margin** (δ stated on the card before data), never a non-significant p. — Lakens 2017 [A]; MASAI non-inferiority [A]; Benavoli et al. 2017 ROPE [A].
14. **Zero-error claims carry the exact one-sided binomial floor**, and the card states how many flawless items the promised precision needs (99.4% → 498). — Hanley & Lippman-Hand 1983 [A]; Clopper-Pearson 1934.
15. **Reliability reported as pass^k, not only mean**, for the tool-call round trip; K and seeds/temperature fixed and published; "don't touch the thermostat". — τ-bench [B]; Miller §3.3 [B]; BetterBench seeds/temperature [A].
16. **Contamination status and a private/held-out split**: which items are public, which are held out, who has seen the held-out answers (including the vendor), item creation dates so post-release scoring is possible. — HLE [A]; LiveCodeBench "scrolling through time" [A]; Balloccu et al. leak severity bands [A]; Xu et al. taxonomy [B].
17. **Versioning and re-grading rules**: every fix bumps a version; old results are declared non-comparable and re-graded; a tag reproduces old behaviour; a changelog names each fixed item. — tau2-bench v1.0.1 note [B]; OpenAI retirement post [B].
18. **Provenance and money on the card**: who built it, on behalf of which entity, who funded it, who pays the benchmark authors (customers among the evaluated), how many internal variants were run before release, no retraction of the vendor's own submitted scores, deprecation criteria, feedback channel, licence, long-term hosting, Croissant metadata. — Gebru et al. Datasheets "Motivation" [C]; Leaderboard Illusion recommendations [A]; Epoch/FrontierMath [B]; BetterBench maintenance criteria [A]; NeurIPS E&D hosting rules [C].

## 3. Three statistical tools to standardise on

**Tool 1 — Paired, clustered standard errors for every comparison (Miller 2024, eqs. 7–8; Abadie et al. 2022).**
Recipe: for item i in cluster c (cluster = case; family if items share a template), d_i = s_A,i − s_B,i. Mean d̄. SE_paired,clustered = (1/n)·[ Σ_c Σ_i Σ_j (d_ic − d̄)(d_jc − d̄) ]^{1/2}. CI95 = d̄ ± 1.96·SE. Report n, number of clusters, d̄, SE, CI, Corr(s_A, s_B). Library: `statsmodels` OLS of d on a constant with `cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": cluster_id}` gives exactly this; or the direct sum above. Power: n = (z_{α/2}+z_β)²·(ω²+σ²_A/K_A+σ²_B/K_B)/δ² (Miller eq. 9; use clustered ω² from Appendix C). For binary per-item outcomes on one test set also print the McNemar table (b = A right/B wrong, c = A wrong/B right) and its exact binomial p (Dietterich 1998; `statsmodels.stats.contingency_tables.mcnemar(exact=True)`). Guard: for any slice with n < ~100 do not use the CLT interval; use Wilson (`statsmodels.stats.proportion.proportion_confint(method="wilson")`) or Clopper-Pearson (`method="beta"`) (Bowyer et al. 2025).

**Tool 2 — TOST equivalence at a pre-registered margin for "no degradation" (Schuirmann 1987; Lakens 2017; clinical form: MASAI).**
Recipe: fix δ (e.g. 3 pp) on the card before data. Compute the paired difference and its (clustered) SE from Tool 1. Two one-sided tests: H0_L: Δ ≤ −δ vs H1: Δ > −δ; H0_U: Δ ≥ +δ vs H1: Δ < +δ; p_TOST = max(p_L, p_U). Equivalent statement: the 90% CI (1−2α at α=0.05) of Δ lies inside (−δ, +δ). Libraries: `statsmodels.stats.weightstats.ttost_paired(x1, x2, low=-δ, upp=+δ)` for continuous per-item scores; `statsmodels.stats.proportion.binom_tost(count, nobs, low, upp)` for a single proportion; R `TOSTER::t_TOST(paired=TRUE, eqb=δ)`. Bayesian alternative with the same reading: posterior Beta(1+Σy, 1+Σ(1−y)) per arm (Bowyer et al. eq. 7) and the mass of the difference inside ±δ (Benavoli et al. 2017 ROPE). Resolution guard: the interval cannot be narrower than the exact binomial allows — print the CP floor beside it (Tool 3). Power at true Δ=0 with per-item difference variance ≈ discordance rate p_disc: n ≈ (z_{0.95}+z_{0.80})²·p_disc/δ² → for δ = 0.03: p_disc 0.05 → 476; 0.10 → 952; 0.20 → 1,903 items (computed here from the standard TOST sample-size formula; verify against Miller's clustered ω² on real data).

**Tool 3 — Exact binomial (Clopper-Pearson) floors for precision and zero-error claims (Clopper & Pearson 1934; Hanley & Lippman-Hand 1983).**
Recipe: with x correct of n, one-sided 95% lower bound = BetaInv(0.05; x, n−x+1) (`scipy.stats.beta.ppf(0.05, x, n-x+1)`; `statsmodels … proportion_confint(x, n, alpha=0.10, method="beta")[0]` for the one-sided 95%). With zero errors the bound is 0.05^{1/n}; rule of three ≈ 1 − 3/n. Required n for a promised precision p at zero errors: n = ⌈ln 0.05 / ln p⌉ → 99.0%: 299; 99.4%: 498; 99.5%: 598; 99.9%: 2,995. Print "observed x/n; certifies ≥ p_lower". Never bootstrap a proportion for this purpose: a percentile bootstrap over identical outcomes is zero-width at any n (Efron & Tibshirani 1993 describes the resampling; Miller calls the bootstrap unnecessary here; Bowyer et al. show CLT/bootstrap under-coverage at small n).

## 4. Disclosure text a vendor-authored benchmark should carry, with precedent

Suggested card text (adapt names):

> **Provenance and conflict of interest.** This benchmark was designed and is maintained by <Vendor>, which also sells <product>, a system this benchmark evaluates. <Vendor> funded its creation; no other party funded it. <N> employees of <Vendor> wrote or reviewed items; <M> external reviewers with no commercial relationship to <Vendor> reviewed <K>% of items. The item-selection rule is <rule>; it can be computed without any masker and was fixed before <product> was run on the corpus. <X>% of item values fall inside <Vendor>'s own dictionaries; scores are reported separately inside and outside that set. Before public release <Vendor> ran <V> internal versions of <product> against this corpus; none of those runs is withdrawn and all are listed at <link>. A held-out split of <H> items exists whose answers <Vendor>'s engineering team has not seen; it is scored by <third party> and results are published unchanged. Any correction to items bumps the version; results across versions are not comparable and are re-graded. <Vendor> commits not to retract its own submitted scores. Other vendors' systems were run with the same harness, prompts and seeds; those vendors were <invited / not invited> to review their runs before publication. Money: <Vendor> receives no payment from evaluated parties for inclusion; <Vendor> <does / does not> have customers among evaluated parties.

Precedent for each clause:
- "who built it / who funded it" — Gebru et al. Datasheets, Motivation section ("Who created the dataset and on behalf of which entity? … Who funded the creation of the dataset?") [C]; the FrontierMath case shows the cost of answering these late [B].
- "internal variants run before release, none withdrawn" — Leaderboard Illusion: "Providers should also disclose the total number of private variants tested prior to public launch, including historical submissions" and "Prohibit score retraction after submission" [A].
- "held-out split the vendor has not seen, scored by a third party" — HLE private set [A]; Epoch's 50-question holdout [B]; Scale SEAL "collaborate with trusted third party organizations to help review our work" [B].
- "selection rule independent of the masker; dictionary-overlap strata" — Bras et al. [A]; Lewis et al. [A].
- "version bump, re-grade, no cross-version comparison" — tau2-bench v1.0.1 note [B]; OpenAI SWE-bench Verified retirement [B].
- "same harness and prompts for every vendor" — Vals AI statement [D]; HELM standardization [A].
- "we will retire it when it stops measuring" — OpenAI, Feb 2026: "This is why we have stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified scores, and we recommend that other model developers do so too." [B]

What made vendor benchmarks distrusted, in one line each: score chosen after the fact (Arena private variants, 27 for one launch); funder holds the answers and contributors were not told (FrontierMath); the evaluator ranks its own customers without saying so (SEAL/Vals — a gap, not a scandal); the vendor keeps reporting a number it knows is contaminated (SWE-bench Verified until Feb 2026 — the cure was to say so).

## 5. Gaps and things not found

- No LLM-benchmark paper using TOST/equivalence testing was found; the precedent chain is Schuirmann → Lakens → MASAI (clinical AI) and Benavoli's ROPE (classifier comparison). State this in the design doc.
- BetterBench's Appendix I checklist was not retrieved verbatim (paywalled PDF extract failed); the criteria quoted above come from the paper body and the project site.
- Datasheets questions are quoted from adopters reproducing the paper's list, not from the CACM text itself.
- Model-in-the-loop as a *named* pattern comes from adversarial filtering (Zellers 2018, Bras 2020) and Dynabench (Kiela 2021, not fetched); "criterion contamination" is borrowed from psychometrics and should be presented as an analogy, not a term of art in ML.
- Artificial Analysis' funding disclosure was not located in this search; do not cite it as a model of independence without reading its methodology page.
